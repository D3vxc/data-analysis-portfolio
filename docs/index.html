What happens when the attack is new?

A detector trained on known attacks is good at known attacks. The question my dissertation asks is what it does when something arrives that nobody has labelled. The proposed answer is a hybrid: pair the trained model with an anomaly detector that has only ever seen normal traffic. I built it, and then I tried to break it.

  1. 01

    6,000 flows the system never saw

    Each dot is one connection from the held-out test half of UNSW-NB15. The orange ones are attacks: 55.1% of them, shuffled at random.

  2. 02

    Ranked by how suspicious they look

    An autoencoder is trained on normal traffic alone and scores every flow by how badly it reconstructs it. It never sees a single attack label and still reaches 0.90 ROC AUC. The labelled model then ranks everything. Keep scrolling and the attacks rise.

  3. 03

    Everything above the line gets flagged

    At the usual threshold the hybrid catches 98.3% of the attacks. Taken alone that number means very little, which is what the rest of this page is about.

  4. 04

    Now split it by what the traffic actually was

    Nearly every attack is caught. The cost sits in the top band: 26.4% of perfectly normal traffic is flagged too. On a network carrying millions of connections a day that is an unusable number of false alarms, and no ROC AUC of 0.98 will tell you so.

The hybrid only wins before the labels arrive

ROC AUC against the number of labelled flows the supervised half was trained on, five draws each. The anomaly detector needs no labels at all, so its score is a flat line.

With 50 labelled flows the hybrid gains 0.020 AUC. By 250 the gap is noise, and on the full training set it is -0.0002. This is the dissertation's real finding, and it is not the one the title hoped for.

Taking a whole attack family out of training

Each row retrains all three models with that family deleted, then tests on it. If the hybrid were going to earn its keep anywhere, it would be here. It does not: every row is within 0.002 of the supervised model. The interesting row is Fuzzers, caught just 21.9% of the time when held out, because fuzzing looks like ordinary traffic at the flow level and the anomaly detector is no better at it.

Second case · built alongside it

Who misses next month's payment?

A card issuer wants to reach customers before they default, but its team can only call one in five. The model has to rank well, give a reason for every flag, and treat groups fairly. Built on 30,000 real customer records with XGBoost and SHAP. Calling the riskiest 20% reaches 51% of next month's defaults, and 56.5% of those called do default, 2.6 times the average rate.

Leaving age out of the model did not make its outcomes equal. Under-30s are flagged most and 30 to 39s least, a ratio of 0.80, right on the four-fifths screening line. Fine for a supportive phone call; it would need fixing before the score could limit anyone's credit.

How I check my own work

  • Validated

    Validate the data before modelling it

    Every analysis opens with a data-quality table. The retail data lost nearly 3% of its rows to duplicates and cancellations before any number was reported.

  • Measured

    Choose the metric that matches the decision

    Accuracy hid a failing intrusion detector. For credit I report capture at a fixed call capacity, because that is what the team actually controls.

  • Tested

    Test the experiment, not just the result

    The A/B test result was significant, and the split between groups was also off. Both went into the recommendation.

  • Explained

    Explain it and look for unequal outcomes

    Reason codes for every prediction, calibration by score band, and error rates by sex and age before a model is called finished.
